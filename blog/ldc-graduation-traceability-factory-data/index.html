LDC graduation and buyer traceability: why factory data is now a business asset

RAED Technologies·17 October 2026·6 min read

For decades, Bangladesh's garment exporters have competed on price, capacity and reliability. Two shifts are now adding a fourth factor: proof. Graduation from Least Developed Country (LDC) status will change the trade preferences that much of the industry was built on, and buyers in the EU and the US are under growing legal pressure to show where their products come from and how they were made. Both point in the same direction. The factories that can produce accurate, timely data about materials, processes and people will be easier to buy from. Factory data is turning from an administrative by-product into a business asset.

This article sets out what is confirmed, what is still pending, and what a factory can sensibly do now. Dates and facts are taken from the sources listed at the end; where something is not final, we say so.

LDC graduation: where things stand

The UN General Assembly decided in November 2021 that Bangladesh would graduate from the LDC category after a five-year preparatory period. That puts the scheduled graduation date at 24 November 2026.

In February 2026, the government asked the UN to extend the preparatory period by three years, to 24 November 2029. According to reporting in Bangladesh's business press, the UN Committee for Development Policy recommended that the request be considered positively, the Economic and Social Council forwarded it, and the General Assembly's Second Committee was scheduled to discuss it in October 2026. The final decision rests with the General Assembly and is expected before 24 November 2026. At the time of writing, we have not seen confirmation of the outcome.

Either way, graduation is a question of when, not whether. A deferral would give more time to prepare; it would not remove the need to.

What graduation means for EU market access

Bangladeshi exports currently enter the EU duty-free and quota-free under the Everything But Arms (EBA) scheme, which is reserved for LDCs. After graduation, the EU provides a three-year transition before EBA access ends.

The EU's new Generalised Scheme of Preferences regulation, Regulation (EU) 2026/1395, was published in June 2026 and is scheduled to apply from 1 January 2027 for ten years. Its enhanced tier, GSP+, requires beneficiary countries to ratify and effectively implement a list of international conventions on human and labour rights, environment and good governance. Commentators have raised concerns that the regulation's safeguard thresholds for apparel could limit the benefit for Bangladeshi garments; how this works out in practice is still being debated.

The common thread for factories is clear: future preferential access is tied more closely to demonstrable compliance than it was under EBA.

Buyer traceability: the regulatory pull

Separately from graduation, buyers' own obligations are expanding. Three examples matter for apparel exporters:

  • EU Forced Labour Regulation. Regulation (EU) 2024/3015 prohibits placing products made with forced labour on the EU market. It applies from 14 December 2027. The European Commission published guidelines on its application in June 2026. Importers will need to be able to show where products and their inputs came from.
  • EU Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR). Regulation (EU) 2024/1781 introduces a framework for Digital Product Passports. Textiles and apparel are a priority product group in the Commission's working plan. The specific requirements for textiles, including what product information must be recorded, will be set in a future delegated act that has not yet been adopted.
  • US Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act. In force since June 2022, it allows US Customs and Border Protection to detain goods unless importers can show, with documentation, that their supply chains are free of forced labour from the specified region. Cotton supply chains have been a focus.

None of these regulations is addressed to Bangladeshi factories directly. They are addressed to the brands and importers who buy from them. In practice, those buyers pass the evidence requirement up the supply chain, through questionnaires, audits, portal uploads and contract clauses.

Buyers are not only asking whether you comply. Increasingly, they are asking you to prove it, order by order.

Why this makes factory data valuable

Consider the questions a buyer may ask about a single shipment:

  • Which fabric lots went into these cartons, and from which supplier?
  • Where did the yarn or fibre in that fabric come from?
  • Which lines and which subcontractors handled the order?
  • What were the working hours on those lines during production?
  • What tests were done, and where are the reports?

A factory that answers these from records joined up by order, lot, bundle and carton can respond quickly and consistently. A factory that answers from separate registers, emails and spreadsheets will be slower, will make more mistakes, and may struggle to answer at all. Over time, buyers allocate orders accordingly. Data becomes part of what you sell.

What a factory can do now

You do not need to wait for final rules to start. The core capabilities are the same whatever the details turn out to be.

1. Map your material chain

Document, for your main product lines, the path from fibre or yarn supplier to finished carton. Note where records exist, in what form, and who holds them. Gaps on this map are your priorities.

2. Collect supplier documentation systematically

Transaction certificates, mill declarations, test reports and origin documents should be linked to the specific lots received, not filed loosely by supplier. Ask your fabric and yarn suppliers what they can provide, and make it part of purchasing.

3. Capture movement on the floor

Link roll to lay, lay to bundle, bundle to garment, garment to carton. Scanning at defined points (receiving, cutting, line entry and exit, packing) builds this chain as work happens rather than reconstructing it later.

4. Keep labour records reliable and connected

Attendance, working hours and payroll records should be accurate, tamper-evident and retrievable by date and line. These are central to any forced-labour inquiry.

5. Make subcontracting visible

Unrecorded subcontracting is one of the most common traceability breaks. Record every outside process (washing, printing, embroidery) against the order and lot.

6. Prepare to share data in standard formats

Buyers will want data in their portals and formats. Systems that can export structured data, or connect through APIs, will save repeated manual work.

A readiness checklist

  • Can you trace a finished carton back to its fabric lots within an hour?
  • Are supplier certificates linked to the lots they cover?
  • Are all subcontracted processes recorded against orders?
  • Are attendance and hours records digital, accurate and auditable?
  • Is there a named person responsible for traceability data?
  • Could you export an order's full history in a structured format if a buyer asked?
