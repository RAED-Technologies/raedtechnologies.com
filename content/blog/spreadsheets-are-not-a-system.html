Excel built more businesses than any software vendor will admit. It is flexible, familiar and free to start. But a spreadsheet is a tool for one person to think with, not a system for fifty people to run a company on. The trouble is that the shift happens gradually. One file becomes ten, ten become a shared folder, and one day the monthly report depends on a single person who knows which tab to copy where. This article helps you recognise when you have reached that point, and what to do about it without a risky big-bang project.

Why spreadsheets break as you grow

Spreadsheets are not the problem. The problem is using them for jobs they were never designed to do:

  • Many people editing the same data. Spreadsheets have no reliable way of controlling who changes what, or of preventing two people from overwriting each other.
  • Data that must stay consistent across departments. When sales, production and accounts each keep their own copy, the copies drift apart.
  • Rules that must always be followed. A formula can be overwritten by a typed number, and nobody will notice.
  • A history you can audit. Spreadsheets record the latest value, not who changed it, when and why.

Eight signs you have outgrown Excel

If three or more of these are familiar, it is time to plan a change.

  1. "Which version is the latest?" Files named final, final2 and final-updated circulate by email or messaging apps, and decisions are made on the wrong one.
  2. One person is the system. Only one person understands how a critical file works. When they are away, the report is late or wrong.
  3. Month-end takes days of copying. Staff spend more time collecting and reconciling sheets than analysing what they show.
  4. The same data is typed twice or more. An order is entered in the sales sheet, again in the production plan and again in the invoice.
  5. Numbers disagree in meetings. Sales and production arrive with different figures for the same order, and the meeting becomes a reconciliation exercise.
  6. Approvals happen in chat. Purchase approvals, price changes and dispatch decisions are given in messages that cannot be traced later.
  7. Files are slow, large or breaking. Workbooks take minutes to open, links to other files fail, and formulas return errors nobody can explain.
  8. Customers or auditors ask questions you cannot answer quickly. "When did this change?" "Who approved this?" "Show me the history of this order."

When the business depends on knowing which file is the real one, you no longer have a spreadsheet. You have an undocumented system with no controls.

What a "system" gives you that a spreadsheet does not

  • One source of truth. Each order, item, customer or supplier exists once, and everyone sees the same record.
  • Roles and permissions. People see and change only what their job requires.
  • Enforced rules. Required fields, valid values and approval steps happen every time, not when someone remembers.
  • History and audit trail. Every change is recorded with a name and a time.
  • Live reporting. Reports read directly from current data, so month-end becomes a review rather than a project.

What to do next: a practical path

Step 1: Map your spreadsheets

List every spreadsheet that matters to daily operations. For each one, note who owns it, who uses it, where its data comes from, where its data goes, and how often it is updated. Most companies are surprised by the length of this list. It is also the best map of your real processes you will ever have.

Step 2: Find the most painful flow

Do not try to replace everything. Look for the flow where errors are most expensive or rework is highest. Common candidates:

  • Order to dispatch: from customer order to production plan to delivery.
  • Purchase to payment: requisitions, approvals, receiving and supplier bills.
  • Inventory: stock in, stock out, and what is really on the shelf.

Step 3: Decide build, buy or combine

For common processes such as accounting, a standard product is usually right. For the flow that is specific to how your business works, a focused custom application may fit better. Many businesses end up with both, connected through integrations so data is entered once.

Step 4: Clean the data before you move it

Migrating messy data into a new system just produces a tidy-looking mess. Agree master lists (customers, suppliers, items, units of measure) and clean duplicates before go-live. This step is tedious and essential.

Step 5: Run in parallel, briefly, then switch

Run the new system alongside the spreadsheet for a short, fixed period, compare results, fix differences, then retire the spreadsheet for that flow. Set the switch date in advance and keep to it. Permanent parallel running doubles the work and halves the trust.

Step 6: Keep Excel for what it is good at

Spreadsheets remain excellent for analysis, modelling and one-off questions. The goal is not to ban them. It is to make sure they read from the system, rather than being the system.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Copying the spreadsheet into software screen by screen. Use the move to simplify. Many columns exist only to work around spreadsheet limits.
  • Leaving out the people who actually use the files. The clerk who maintains the sheet knows its exceptions better than anyone.
  • Trying to replace every file at once. Phase the change, one flow at a time, so each step delivers value and builds confidence.
  • Forgetting training and ownership. A system needs a named business owner, not just an IT contact.

A quick self-check

  • Could a new manager find the current status of any order without asking a person?
  • If your key spreadsheet owner left tomorrow, would operations continue normally?
  • Can you show who approved the last ten purchases, and when?
  • Do sales, production and accounts agree on this month's numbers without a meeting?

Each "no" is a specific, solvable problem, and a good place to start.
