Managing rental property looks simple from the outside: collect rent, fix what breaks, keep owners informed. In practice, a landlord or property manager with a few buildings is juggling dozens of tenancies with different start dates, advances, service charges, utility arrangements and maintenance requests, usually across notebooks, spreadsheets, phone calls and messaging groups.

Property management software brings these strands together. This article explains what such a system should do for rent, maintenance and owner reporting, how to judge whether you need one, and how to move to it without confusing tenants or owners.

Who this is for

The same principles apply to several kinds of business:

  • individual landlords or families with several buildings;
  • property management companies acting for many owners;
  • developers who manage handed-over apartments and common areas;
  • owners of commercial space, shops and offices with multiple tenants.

The details differ. A commercial tenancy may have annual escalations and service-charge budgets, while a residential building may have simpler monthly rent. The structure of a good system is the same.

The core: units, tenancies and money

Get the property structure right

Model your portfolio as it really is: owners, properties, buildings, floors and units, plus shared areas such as parking, rooftops and generators. Each unit should have its own record, so you can see its tenancy history, maintenance history and income over time.

Record every tenancy properly

For each tenancy, record the parties, start and end dates, rent, advance or deposit, service charges, payment due date, escalation terms and notice period. Attach the signed agreement. When terms change, record the change with an effective date, rather than overwriting the old figure.

Let the system create the dues

Once tenancies are recorded, the system should generate monthly dues automatically: rent, service charge, utilities where you recharge them, and any agreed adjustments. This removes the most common source of disputes, which is a manually prepared bill that differs from the agreement.

Rent collection: clear, recorded, reconciled

  • Send dues before they are due. A short message or email a few days before the due date, with the amount and how to pay.
  • Accept the ways tenants actually pay: bank transfer, mobile wallet, cheque or cash, each recorded against the right tenancy.
  • Issue a receipt for every payment, automatically, with a running balance.
  • Track arrears by age. A simple list of what is overdue, by how long, lets you follow up early and consistently.
  • Handle advances and deposits separately, with a clear record of any adjustments at the end of a tenancy.

Consistency matters as much as automation. When every tenant receives the same reminders and receipts, collection becomes a routine rather than a series of awkward conversations.

Most rent disputes are not about money. They are about two people holding different records of what was agreed and what was paid.

Maintenance: from phone calls to tracked requests

Maintenance is where tenant satisfaction is won or lost, and where costs leak without anyone noticing.

  1. One way to report. Tenants report problems through a simple form, link or app, with a photo. Phone reports are entered by staff into the same list.
  2. Categorise and prioritise. A water leak or a lift failure is urgent. A loose cupboard door is not. Agree response targets for each category.
  3. Assign and track. Each request is assigned to a staff member or contractor, with a status the tenant can see.
  4. Record the cost. Materials and labour are recorded against the unit and, where agreed, recharged to the tenant or owner.
  5. Close with confirmation. The tenant confirms the work is done, or the request reopens.

Add planned maintenance for shared equipment such as lifts, generators, pumps and fire safety systems, with reminders before each service is due. Over time, the maintenance history tells you which units and which equipment cost the most to keep running.

Owner reporting: trust through transparency

If you manage property for others, owner reporting is your product. Owners want to know, without asking:

  • which units are let, vacant or due for renewal;
  • rent billed, collected and outstanding, by unit;
  • maintenance carried out and what it cost;
  • your management fee and the net amount paid to them.

A monthly statement generated from the same records your team uses every day, plus a secure online view, replaces the spreadsheet that someone assembles at month-end. It also protects you: when questions arise, the history is there.

Signs you need a proper system

  • You cannot produce a list of overdue rent, by tenant, in a few minutes.
  • Tenancy renewals or escalations are missed until a tenant points it out.
  • Maintenance requests are lost in messaging groups.
  • Owner statements take days to prepare and are sometimes corrected afterwards.
  • Only one person knows the true position of each building.

Packaged or custom?

Many packaged property management products exist, and some will fit well. Check whether they handle the way you actually work: local payment methods, Bangla communication for tenants, advance and deposit practices, service charges for shared facilities, and owner statements in the format your owners expect. A custom or extended system makes sense when your portfolio mixes residential and commercial units, when you manage for many owners with different arrangements, or when you need to connect to existing accounting software.

A phased path

  1. Phase 1: record properties, units and tenancies, generate dues and issue receipts.
  2. Phase 2: maintenance requests with tracking and costs.
  3. Phase 3: owner statements and the owner portal.
  4. Phase 4: tenant self-service for payments, receipts and requests.

Before switching, reconcile each tenant's balance with them and agree a starting figure. Starting from agreed balances avoids months of disputes.
